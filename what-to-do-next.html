👉 Decision guide — three pathways

What to Do Next

You have run Bootstrap CUSUM on your data. The result points you to one of three pathways. Each one leads to a different decision and a different next action. This page gives you the complete decision guide for each outcome — including the pathway that most improvement tools skip entirely: what to do when nothing has changed.

✅ Path A
Change detected
A structural shift confirmed at your confidence level
⚠ Path B
No change detected
No structural shift found — the critical pathway
❌ Path C
Data problem
Result unreliable — fix the data and re-run
✅ Path A

Change detected

Bootstrap CUSUM has found a structural change point in your data series — a permanent shift in the process mean at a specific date, confirmed at your chosen confidence level. This is strong evidence that something in the system changed. Now the work is to understand what, why, and whether it is sustained.

  • 1
    Note the change point date precisely. The date the Bootstrap CUSUM identifies is the point at which the accumulated evidence of change becomes statistically significant. The actual cause will typically predate this by weeks or months — that is normal. Your investigation window is the period leading up to the change point date, not the date itself.
  • 2
    Determine the direction. Is this a change you wanted (improvement) or did not want (deterioration)? An upward change point in harm events or complaints is a warning, not a success. A downward change point in error rates or waiting times is what success looks like. Make sure you are reading the direction correctly before drawing any conclusions.
  • 3
    Investigate what changed in the system at that time. What was different about the system in the period leading up to the change point? Policy changes, staffing changes, process redesigns, external events — all are candidates. List everything that changed around that time. The Bootstrap CUSUM narrows the investigation window; your knowledge of context provides the explanation. Use 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram to trace the cause.
  • 4
    Check whether the change is sustained. Run Bootstrap CUSUM on the full series including all available post-change-point data. A temporary excursion followed by return to the previous level is not a structural improvement. Sustained change at the new level, confirmed by the Bootstrap CUSUM continuing to accumulate evidence, is what you are looking for.
  • 5
    If the change was caused by your intervention: confirm the mechanism. Why did this intervention work when others did not? What was different about it — was it at a higher Joiner level? Did it address the dominant constraint? Document the causal mechanism, not just the correlation between the intervention and the change point. That documentation is what makes the learning transferable.
  • 6
    If the change was not caused by your intervention: treat this as important information. Something else changed the system — find out what. Understanding unintended changes is often more valuable than understanding intended ones, because it reveals what the system actually responds to. Check whether your intended intervention has yet produced its own change point — it may simply not have appeared yet.
  • 7
    If this was a deterioration change point: return to Step 1 of the 7-step method. List the symptoms. Find the root cause of the deterioration. Identify the constraint. Design a structural fix. Do not tamper by launching multiple simultaneous responses.
Re-run the tool regularly

A confirmed improvement change point is not a reason to stop monitoring. Run Bootstrap CUSUM on the same outcome measure at regular intervals — every six months is a reasonable cadence for most improvement programmes. Sustained improvement should continue to show the same change point. A new downward change point after a period of improvement is an early warning of regression — and the earlier it is detected, the more options you have.

⚠ Path B — The critical pathway

No change detected — decide deliberately, do not tamper

Bootstrap CUSUM has found no statistical evidence of a structural shift in your data series. The system is producing the same distribution of results it was producing before. This is the most common result for improvement interventions — and the result that is most often mishandled.

The wrong response is to immediately launch a new initiative. The right response is to decide deliberately from the three options below.

⏰ Option 1 — Your key differentiator

Decide to wait — with a pre-committed review date

If you believe the intervention was structurally correct but may not yet have had time to work, the right decision is to wait — deliberately, with a specific review date pre-committed and documented.

This is not passive. It is an active decision that prevents tampering and protects the intervention from political pressure to “do something.” To make this decision defensible, document the following before the review date:

  1. The intervention that was made and when
  2. The outcome measure being tracked and the current Bootstrap CUSUM result
  3. The expected mechanism by which the intervention should produce a change point
  4. The review date (typically 6–12 months for system-level changes)
  5. The pre-committed decision rule: “If no change point appears by [date], we will return to Step 3 of the 7-step method”

This pre-commitment is what makes the wait decision honest. Without it, “wait” becomes indefinite delay. With it, waiting is a disciplined hypothesis: the intervention should work by this mechanism, and we will know by this date whether it did. See Tampering & Impatience for why this matters.

  • 2
    Option 2 — Return to Step 3: revisit the root cause analysis. If the review date has passed and no change point has appeared, the intervention did not address the structural root cause. Return to Step 3 of the 7-step method. Ask: did the root cause analysis stop too early? Did it stop at “human error” rather than the system condition that made the error easy to commit? Was the Joiner test applied to the proposed fix? Was the fix at Level 1 (output) or Level 2 (process) rather than Level 3 (system)? A flat Bootstrap CUSUM after a genuine implementation effort is almost always a root cause analysis problem, not an implementation problem.
  • 3
    Option 3 — Improve the measure. If you are confident the root cause analysis was correct but the Bootstrap CUSUM is not detecting a change, the measure may be wrong. Is this an outcome measure or a process measure? A process that improves without producing a change in the outcome measure has improved locally but not systemically. Check: does this measure capture what the system is actually for? If the measure is a proxy, is it a valid proxy? See Types of measures for the full framework.
Why “no change detected” is the most valuable result

A flat Bootstrap CUSUM line after an intervention is honest information that most measurement systems cannot provide. Standard before-and-after comparisons will almost always find some improvement — because the comparison window is chosen to show one. Bootstrap CUSUM is indifferent to the narrative. It finds what the data contains. A flat line tells you the system has not changed, which tells you to look harder at the cause, not harder at the intervention. Organisations that use Bootstrap CUSUM consistently develop a much clearer understanding of what actually changes their systems — because they stop receiving credit for changes that did not occur.

❌ Path C

Data not usable — fix and re-run

The Bootstrap CUSUM result is unreliable because of a problem with the data series. This is not a failure — it is a diagnostic. The table below maps the most common data problems to their fixes.

Problem Fix
Fewer than 20 data points Extend the historical series if available. If not, reduce frequency — use quarterly instead of monthly to get more historical points. As a last resort, use annual data, which removes seasonal variation and extends effective history.
Missing values (gaps in the series) For occasional gaps (1–2 points), interpolate using the average of the surrounding values. For longer gaps, split the series and run Bootstrap CUSUM on each continuous segment separately. Do not fill gaps with zeros unless zero is a genuine observed value.
Date format not recognised Use ISO format: YYYY-MM-DD (e.g. 2024-03-01). Avoid formats that are ambiguous between UK and US date conventions (03/04/2024 could be 3 April or 4 March). If using monthly data, use the first day of each month as the date.
Mixed frequencies (some monthly, some quarterly) Standardise to a single frequency before running. If you have quarterly data for some periods and monthly for others, either aggregate everything to quarterly or split the series at the point where frequency changes.
Extreme outliers distorting the baseline Identify the outlier and its cause. If the outlier is a genuine one-off event unrelated to the system (e.g. COVID lockdown period), consider running Bootstrap CUSUM on the series excluding that period, and note the exclusion explicitly. Never remove outliers without documenting why.
Series too short after an intervention If the intervention was recent (less than 6 months ago), there may simply not be enough post-intervention data for Bootstrap CUSUM to detect a change point even if one exists. Set a review date and re-run in 6 months. Do not interpret “too soon to tell” as “no change.”
Denominator changes mid-series If the measure is a rate (e.g. events per 1,000 patients) and the denominator changed (e.g. patient population grew), the series is not directly comparable across the denominator change. Recalculate all values using a consistent denominator, or split the series at the point of change.
  • 1
    Fix the specific problem identified above. Make only the minimum change needed to produce a usable series. Document every change you make to the data and why.
  • 2
    Re-run Bootstrap CUSUM on the corrected series. The result on the corrected series is your real result. If it shows a change point, go to Path A. If it shows no change, go to Path B.
  • 3
    Consider whether the measure itself is the problem. Sometimes a data quality problem reveals a deeper issue: the measure was never properly operationally defined, the collection process was inconsistent, or the measure was a proxy that no longer reflects what you care about. See Types of measures for the framework for choosing and defining outcome measures that will be reliable over time.
