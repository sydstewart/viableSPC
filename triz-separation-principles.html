Meet conflicting needs through separation

Right intervention? A practical use of TRIZ separation principles.

Sometimes a proposed improvement helps one need but harms another. Before accepting a compromise, ask: must both needs be met in the same place, at the same time and in the same way?

TRIZ is a collection of tools for inventive problem solving. Separation helps us generate ways to meet apparently conflicting needs by changing how the work is arranged.

One problem, two legitimate needs

Imagine a service business: customers need flexibility, while the delivery team needs repeatable, reliable work. The offer seems to need to be both customised and standard.

Rather than deciding which side must give way, explore whether different parts of the work need different arrangements.

Try four separation questions

  • Time—could the needs be met at different times? Agree customer-specific choices during planning, before routine delivery begins.
  • Space—could the needs be met in different places or parts of the operation? Give customer discussions a dedicated planning area or team, while protecting routine delivery from interruptions.
  • Condition—could different circumstances trigger different arrangements? Use a standard route for routine orders and a defined specialist route for genuine exceptions.
  • System—could the whole and its parts have different properties? Keep a common core service, with configurable options that give individual customers flexibility.

These are possible ideas to explore, not proven fixes. They can overlap. Check that an idea really meets both needs rather than hiding the conflict or moving the burden elsewhere.

Turn an idea into a workable intervention

  • State both needs and why they matter. Involve the people doing the work and those affected. Which assumption makes the needs seem incompatible?
  • Choose an arrangement to test. What would change in practice? Check the authority, capacity, information and resources required—including any new handovers or delays.
  • Predict and check the result. Agree what should improve, by how much and when. Check implementation, outcomes and possible harm elsewhere.

Not every trade-off can be removed. Separation is a way to develop and test possibilities—not a guarantee of a cost-free solution. A chart can help assess performance changes, but cannot establish their cause.

Where do TRIZ’s 40 inventive principles fit?

The 40 principles are more specific prompts for generating ideas. They can help develop a separation—for example, Segmentation suggests dividing something into parts, while Prior Action suggests preparing something in advance.

They are related tools, not 40 extra steps to complete or a rigid one-principle-to-one-separation checklist. Start with the four questions above; use further prompts only if helpful.
