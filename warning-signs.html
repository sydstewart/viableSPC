Major failures rarely come out of the blue

The warning signs are usually there. The difficulty is telling which ones are real, before they are explained away.

Major failures rarely come out of the blue. The warning signs are usually there, in our own near-misses or in someone else’s past accident, and they get explained away.

On this page: What the evidence shows · Not only accidents · Two kinds of warning · Why warnings get explained away · What to do when a warning arrives · Questions people ask

Look back at almost any major failure, whether a sudden accident, years of poor care, or a business in decline, and the same pattern appears. Before the event, there were signs: a worrying number, a recurring fault, a complaint, a near-miss, a similar accident elsewhere. Each was noticed by someone. Each was explained away: “it’s the data”, “it’s a one-off”, “that always happens”, “it couldn’t happen here”.

What the evidence shows

In Man-made Disasters (1978), the sociologist Barry Turner studied a series of disasters and found that each was preceded by an incubation period: a time when events at odds with accepted beliefs about safety built up, unnoticed or misunderstood, until something triggered the failure. Public inquiries since then have found the same thing again and again:

FailureWarning signs beforehand
Challenger space shuttle, 1986Erosion of the booster O-ring seals on earlier flights
Hillsborough, 1989A crush at the same end of the ground at a semi-final in 1981
Columbia space shuttle, 2003Foam striking the shuttle on earlier launches
Texas City refinery, 2005Earlier incidents, and known faults in level instruments and alarms
Mid Staffordshire hospitals, to 2009High mortality alerts, and families’ complaints and campaigning
Morecambe Bay maternity services, to 2013Deaths and family concerns over several years before the problems were acknowledged
Grenfell Tower, 2017Residents’ fire safety concerns, and the Lakanal House fire of 2009
Bird’s triangle: near-misses are free lessons

The pattern holds at smaller scales too. In 1969, Frank Bird analysed about 1.75 million accident reports from 297 companies. For every serious injury he found roughly 10 minor injuries, 30 incidents causing property damage and 600 near-misses, building on Herbert Heinrich’s earlier version of 1931. The conclusion: serious accidents sit on a large base of smaller events, so near-misses show the weaknesses before anyone is hurt.

There is an important caution. The ratios are not fixed laws, and reducing minor incidents does not necessarily prevent major ones when they have different causes. BP’s Texas City refinery had good personal-injury rates while its process-safety risks grew. So the lesson is sharper than “count the near-misses”: watch the near-misses that could lead to the failure you most fear.

Not only accidents

The same pattern applies well beyond sudden accidents. There are at least three kinds of major failure:

Kind of failureExampleHow the warnings look
Sudden harmTexas City, Challenger, GrenfellNear-misses, faulty instruments, earlier similar events
Slow-burn harmCorridor care in emergency departmentsLong waits and crowding every day, complaints, staff concerns
Decline in performance or businessICI’s long decline; Kodak; NokiaFalling margins, lost customers, rivals pulling ahead

Slow-burn harm and decline are in some ways the most dangerous: their warnings are constant, so they become part of the background. See slow-burn harm and decline: when warnings become background. And some of the most important warnings come even earlier, at the moment a big decision is made: see warnings at the moment of decision.

Two kinds of warning

Our own near-misses

The recurring fault, the alarm everyone ignores, the workaround that has become normal, the measure that has quietly drifted. Each one shows that a condition in the system is still there, and the next occurrence may be the one that matters.

Someone else’s accident

Trevor Kletz, who led safety work in ICI’s petrochemicals division for many years, argued that there are no new accidents: the same things have happened before, for similar reasons, but the people involved in the latest one did not know about the earlier lessons. His book Lessons from Disaster: How Organisations Have No Memory and Accidents Recur (1993) shows how even one company can repeat its own accidents once the people who remember them have moved on.

So when we read about a failure elsewhere, the useful question is not “how could they let that happen?” but “could this happen here, and if so, how would we stop it?”

Why warnings get explained away

  • Most warnings are false alarms. Real signals are mixed in with many that come to nothing, so dismissing them usually turns out to be right, until it isn’t.
  • A comfortable explanation is always available. “It’s the data” or “it’s a one-off” costs nothing today; investigating costs time and may bring bad news.
  • Success breeds confidence. “We’ve been fine” is not the same as “we are fine”. A good record is a reason to keep looking, not to stop, and a good headline figure can hide a growing risk elsewhere (see Bird’s triangle).
  • The abnormal becomes normal. A problem that happens often enough without disaster stops looking like a problem.
  • The messenger pays. If raising bad news is unwelcome, people stop raising it.
  • Organisations forget. The people who learned the last lesson move on, and the lesson goes with them.
A fair caution about hindsight

Inquiries look for warnings after a disaster, and with hindsight the warnings line up neatly. At the time, they were mixed in with many signals that led nowhere. So two things are true at once:

  • “Most major failures had warning signs” is well supported by the evidence.
  • “Most warnings lead to failures” is not. Many warnings are false alarms, and a few failures are genuinely new.

Often there was no single dramatic warning, only signals: data, readings or small events that pointed to the problem when looked at properly. That is why the real challenge is not finding warnings, but telling which ones are real, without either chasing every alarm or dismissing them all.

What to do when a warning arrives

When a worrying number, alert or complaint arrives, three questions help:

  1. Is it real? Check it another way before concluding it is the data, a one-off or normal variation. Go and see.
  2. Does it matter? What is the worst credible outcome if it is real, and is it getting worse?
  3. Decide, and record the decision. Act, escalate, or accept the risk openly, with a named decision and a date to look again. Drifting back to business as usual is not a decision.

For measures that are tracked over time, the first question has a statistical answer: has the measure genuinely shifted, or is it ordinary variation? The same question works in reverse: bright spots are the good news hidden in the same data, and they deserve the same check before they are celebrated.

Questions people ask

Do most disasters have warning signs?

Most major failures investigated by public inquiries had warning signs beforehand, in the form of near-misses, data, complaints or similar accidents elsewhere. Barry Turner called the period when these build up the “incubation period”. With hindsight they look clearer than they did at the time, which is why telling real warnings from false alarms matters.

What did Trevor Kletz mean by “there are no new accidents”?

That accidents repeat: the same failure has usually happened before, somewhere, for similar reasons, but the people involved did not know about the earlier lessons, because organisations have no memory; only people do.

How can you tell whether a warning is real?

Check it another way before dismissing it: look at the measure over time rather than a single result, go and see the work, and ask whether it has happened elsewhere. For data tracked over time, a step change analysis shows whether a shift is genuine or ordinary variation.
