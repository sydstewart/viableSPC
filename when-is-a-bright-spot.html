When Is a Bright Spot a Bright Spot? Five Lessons from England’s Emergency Departments

“Most improved” rankings are a welcome way to celebrate progress. But a bright spot is something others will try to copy, so it needs a harder test: has the level genuinely shifted, has it lasted, and is it more than everyone else’s change?

Each month, rankings of the most-improved NHS emergency departments are shared widely. They usually compare one month with the same month a year earlier. We took five trusts that topped one such list, all with gains of more than ten percentage points in 4-hour performance, and looked at their full published figures over three and a half years. Each told a different story. Only one was a lasting improvement, well beyond the change for England, that others could learn from.

We have not named the trusts. This is about how to read the numbers, not about any team: every emergency department in England is working under heavy pressure, and every one of these trusts has staff doing difficult work well.

Five trusts, five stories

Trust A — a genuine bright spot

For more than two years, Trust A’s main emergency department saw about two-thirds of patients within four hours, month after month. Then it stepped up twice: first to the mid-70s, and some months later to the high 80s. Both steps were well beyond the change for England over the same months, and both lasted. The trust’s own board papers describe concerted work by its teams after a very difficult period, a new leadership partnership with a neighbouring trust to share good practice, and new pathways that helped frail older people get care without an unnecessary trip to the emergency department.

Even here there is a useful question: one of the most-praised new pathways started after the first step, so it cannot be the whole explanation. A bright spot should prompt curiosity about exactly what changed, and when.

Trust B — compared with its worst month

Trust B’s month in the ranking was compared with the same month a year earlier — which turned out to be its lowest month in three years. Across the whole series, its only lasting change at a strict setting was a fall, some years earlier. A recent modest recovery had not reached its earlier level.

Lesson: one month against one month mostly measures how unusual those two months were. An unusually bad month a year ago almost guarantees a “big improvement” now. Statisticians call this regression to the mean; in plain words, extremes tend to be followed by something more ordinary.

Trust C — improving is not the same as good

Trust C shows a genuine, lasting step up, more than England’s change over the same months. That is real progress and deserves credit. But even after the step, it was seeing fewer than half of its patients within four hours, well below the England average.

Lesson: a trust can be among the most improved and still have a long way to go. Both facts belong in the story.

Trust D — check what is being counted

Trust D’s figures jumped by more than twenty points within a couple of months, then became unusually steady. A separate analysis later attributed much of the rise to a change in how one group of patients was recorded. Changes like this are often entirely legitimate — new units and pathways need recording rules — but they move the measure without necessarily changing what patients experience. The trust had made genuine improvements too, so the honest summary is: partly a counting change, partly real progress, and the headline figure alone cannot separate them.

Lesson: when a measure jumps suddenly and then becomes unusually steady, check whether what is being counted has changed before celebrating.

Trust E — a good month is not a new level

Trust E’s month in the ranking was well up on a year earlier, but its 4-hour series shows no lasting change at a strict setting: a good month within its normal range. (A different measure, its 12-hour waits, had genuinely improved some time earlier and held — a quieter success that no monthly ranking would pick up.)

Lesson: a ranking for one month captures that month. A bright spot needs a new level that lasts.

Five questions to ask of any “most improved” claim

  1. Compared with what? One month against the same month last year, or a sustained change in level?
  2. Has the level shifted, and has it lasted? Look at the whole series, not two points in it.
  3. Is it more than everyone else’s change? If England improved by two points, a trust that improved by three has mostly moved with the tide.
  4. Has what is counted changed? New pathways, new units and new recording rules can all move a measure without changing patients’ experience.
  5. Improved, or good? Both matter, but they are different questions.

None of this takes away from rankings that celebrate progress, or from the teams in them. Asking these questions is what turns a ranking into learning: it shows where to look for practices worth copying, and where to look more carefully first.

Why rankings mislead: Deming’s point

None of this is new. W. Edwards Deming argued that most of the variation in any organisation’s results comes from the system people work in, not from the people themselves — in Out of the Crisis he estimated that most problems and opportunities for improvement belong to the system. Ranking people or organisations on their results therefore mostly ranks that ordinary variation: someone always comes top and someone bottom, even when nothing real separates them. He counted rating performance this way among the “deadly diseases” of management, and showed it vividly in his Red Bead Experiment, where workers are praised and blamed for results the system alone decides.

His remedy was to learn to tell common-cause variation, the everyday ups and downs of a stable system, from special causes that signal something has genuinely changed. That is exactly the distinction behind “has the level shifted, and has it lasted?” Two of the five trusts above owed their place in the ranking to common-cause variation: one compared with its worst month, one with a good month within its normal range. Others have carried the idea into public services: David Spiegelhalter’s funnel plots show which differences between hospitals are larger than chance, and NHS England’s Making Data Count programme uses statistical process control charts instead of rankings. See Deming’s 14 Points and Variation and SPC for more.

How we checked

We used NHS England’s published monthly A&E figures for every trust from April 2023 to October 2026, and the free StepChange Analyzer (Bootstrap CUSUM change-point analysis) to find lasting changes in each trust’s type 1 (main emergency department) 4-hour performance, date them, and test whether they held up. Each change was compared with England over the same months and with the same months a year earlier, and checked for signs of a reporting gap or a change in what was counted. How the analyser is tested explains the checks behind the tool. We name a trust as a bright spot only when the trust itself has made the improvement public.
