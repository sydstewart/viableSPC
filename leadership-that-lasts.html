Leadership that lasts: build it into structure, measures and memory

Judge leadership not by what happens while the leader is there, but by what carries on after they leave.

The best leadership is not the most charismatic, or the most successful in one era. It is the leadership that builds a system which keeps refusing “this is the way it is” after the leader has gone. Admiral Rickover’s nuclear navy programme and commercial aviation are often held up as the clearest examples of high reliability organisations: their safety habits have outlasted generations of leaders, because they were built into how those organisations work.

Move each good habit out of people’s heads and into something that runs whether or not anyone remembers.

Trevor Kletz, who led safety work in ICI’s petrochemicals division, put the problem simply: organisations have no memory; only people do. Structure, measures and memory are how an organisation gets a memory of its own.

Structure: who has authority, and what happens automatically

  • A safety role with real authority and a direct line to the top. Kletz at ICI; James Bagian at the US Veterans Health Administration’s National Center for Patient Safety; and, after the Texas City refinery explosion, the US Chemical Safety Board’s recommendation that BP appoint a board member with process safety expertise. If the role can be ignored when it is inconvenient, it is not structural.
  • The right to stop the line, for anyone. Toyota’s andon cord, and Virginia Mason’s patient safety alerts: written down, backed by leaders, and used without penalty.
  • Escalation triggers instead of judgement calls. “Any unexplained change, call the supervisor”; the NHS early warning score. The rule decides, so no one has to feel brave. See simple rules.
  • A review of every significant change. The lesson of Flixborough (1974), where a temporary modification failed: a modification, a software release or a new site does not go ahead without a proportionate review of what could go wrong.
  • Named, time-limited risk acceptance. Not acting needs a name, a reason and an expiry date, so a deferred repair cannot quietly become permanent.
  • Harm as the first item on the leadership agenda. When Paul O’Neill became chief executive of the aluminium company Alcoa in 1987, he opened his first meeting with investors by talking about worker safety, not profit. Safety stayed the first item of business, injury rates fell sharply, and the company prospered. He later became US Treasury Secretary, and worked to improve patient safety in Pittsburgh’s hospitals (see never events). The habit lasts when harm is the standing first item of business, not one leader’s preference.
  • Independent checks built in. A mass balance, a reconciliation, an outside investigation: a second route to the truth that does not depend on the first being believed.

Measures: what gets seen, and how quickly

  • Separate measures for different kinds of risk (everyday harm, major hazards, financial losses), so one good number cannot hide a bad one, as good injury rates did at BP’s Texas City refinery.
  • Leading measures and near-misses, not just outcomes. See Bird’s triangle.
  • Automatic change detection in routine reports, so spotting a real shift does not depend on someone happening to notice. The StepChange Analyzer’s method belongs here: built into the monthly report, not used only occasionally.
  • Balancing measures for every major change, to catch side effects elsewhere.
  • Measures of the leadership itself: the time from a report to action, the share of fixes that redesign the system rather than retrain people, and whether near-miss reporting is rising. These show whether the habits are alive or only on paper.

Memory: what the organisation keeps knowing after people leave

  • Turn lessons into design, not documents. A lesson in a report is recorded. A lesson built into a standard, a checklist, a hazard log or the equipment itself is learned. A drain that has been blanked off remembers what operators forget.
  • For software, turn every incident into an automated test. Each failure becomes a check that runs on every release, for ever. The code cannot forget.
  • Tell the organisation’s own stories. Kletz’s safety newsletters did this; a short set of your own case histories, used in induction, teaches newcomers what went wrong last time and why.
  • Review other people’s accidents routinely. A standing item that asks of each outside incident: could this happen here? See two kinds of warning.
  • Protect memory through change. Reorganisations, mergers and changes of leader are when memory is most easily lost. A handover that covers the key hazards, open risks and stories should be part of every such change.
  • Give memory an owner. Someone, or some role, responsible for keeping the lessons alive: what has been called a modern “remembrancer”. In the UK, the Remembrancer is an ancient office: the King’s Remembrancer, dating from the twelfth century, kept the Exchequer’s records, and the City of London still has a Remembrancer today. The idea is the same: a named person whose job is to remember on the organisation’s behalf.

The test

For each habit you care about, ask: if the person who champions this left tomorrow, would it carry on? If the answer depends on someone’s enthusiasm, it is still in a person. If it is in a role, a rule, a report or a test, it is in the organisation.

Writing down the cases, the questions and the methods is how one person’s way of thinking becomes something that outlasts them.

Questions people ask

How do you make an improvement last after the leader who started it leaves?

Move each good habit out of people's heads and into something that runs whether or not anyone remembers: a role with real authority, a rule or trigger, a routine report or measure, a standard, or an automated test. If a habit depends on one person's enthusiasm, it will usually fade when they go.

What did Trevor Kletz mean by “organisations have no memory”?

That lessons are held by people, not organisations. When the people who learned a lesson move on, the organisation forgets it, and the same accidents recur. The remedy is to build lessons into designs, standards, procedures and training, so the organisation keeps them.
