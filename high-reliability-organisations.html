High reliability organisations: the habits that keep warnings visible

Some organisations do dangerous, complex work for years with remarkably few failures. The question is not why things go wrong, but why they so rarely do.

From the 1980s, researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, including Todd La Porte, Gene Rochlin and Karlene Roberts, studied organisations such as US Navy aircraft carriers, air traffic control and a nuclear power plant. Each ran hazardous operations where a single mistake could be catastrophic, yet serious failures were rare. They called them high reliability organisations.

What made them different was not better equipment or cleverer people, but a set of everyday habits that kept small problems visible before they became large ones. Karl Weick and Kathleen Sutcliffe, in Managing the Unexpected, described five.

The five habits

The first three help an organisation anticipate problems; the last two help it contain them when they happen.

1. Preoccupation with failure

Treat every near-miss and small lapse as a warning about the whole system, not as proof that the defences worked. A good record is a reason to keep looking: “we’ve been fine” is not “we are fine”. See near-misses are free lessons.

2. Reluctance to simplify

Resist the comfortable explanation: “it’s the data”, “a one-off”, “human error”. Look for the conditions behind an event, and welcome people who see it differently. See why warnings get explained away and why root cause analysis isn’t enough.

3. Sensitivity to operations

Know what is actually happening at the front line, now, not what the procedures say should happen. Leaders who see the work for themselves notice the workarounds and drift that reports miss. See going to the gemba.

4. Commitment to resilience

Expect surprises, and be good at catching them early and recovering before they escalate: spare capacity, practised responses, and the ability to improvise. Resilience is also what stops slow-burn problems becoming normal. See slow-burn harm and decline.

5. Deference to expertise

When a problem arises, decisions move to whoever knows most about it, not whoever is most senior. That only works if people feel safe to speak up, and are thanked when they do. See help me lead and sustain improvement.

A sixth habit: learning from others, outside and inside

Weick and Sutcliffe’s five habits are mostly about watching your own operation. We would add a sixth: learning from others, both outside and inside the organisation.

  • Outside: Trevor Kletz argued that there are no new accidents: the same things happen again because the lessons of earlier ones have been forgotten, or were never learned where it mattered. Someone else’s accident is a warning we can learn from without paying its price; the question to ask is “could this happen here, and if so, how would we stop it?” See two kinds of warning.
  • Inside: another ward’s near-miss, another site’s workaround, another team’s incident report: the warning may already be inside the organisation, in a part that does not talk to yours.
  • From success, too: teams that do something unusually well, inside or outside, show what is possible. See bright spots, including Learning from Excellence.

Learning from others only lasts if it is built in: a standing agenda item, a named owner, a routine review of other people’s incidents. See leadership that lasts.

Simple rules for the known; judgement for the unexpected

Alongside the habits, high reliability organisations rely on a few simple, memorable rules for moments when there is no time to think. Wildland firefighters learn LACES (lookouts, communications, escape routes, safety zones) and the Ten Standard Firefighting Orders, drawn up in 1957 after a series of fatal fires. Donald Sull and Kathleen Eisenhardt make the general case in Simple Rules: How to Thrive in a Complex World (2015): in complex, fast-moving situations, a handful of clear rules works better than a thick manual, because people can remember and use them under pressure.

When a rule no longer fits

Simple rules work for known hazards. The unexpected needs judgement too. At the Mann Gulch fire in 1949, the foreman Wagner Dodge survived by lighting an “escape fire” and lying in its ashes, an improvisation his crew did not understand. In his essay Drop Your Tools, Karl Weick described firefighters who died still carrying their heavy tools, because dropping them went against everything they had been trained to do. The lesson, drop your tools when you need to escape, has since become part of firefighter safety thinking: a new simple rule, learned from failure.

So the rules themselves must evolve. Good organisations:

  • Add rules when incidents, here or elsewhere, reveal a hazard the rules did not cover.
  • Review rules regularly, asking whether each still fits how the work is done now.
  • Retire rules that no longer help: a rule nobody understands, or everybody works around, is itself a warning sign.
  • Keep the reasons with the rules, so people know when a rule applies and when the situation has moved beyond it.

This is the sixth habit at work: learning from others, and from our own experience, turned into rules that the organisation keeps. See leadership that lasts.

High reliability in healthcare

In healthcare, “reliability” can mean two different things, and it helps to keep them apart.

Reliable care: the right thing, every time

For patients, reliability first means dependable, repeatable delivery of known good practice: the right medicine at the right dose to the right patient, the test that is due done on time, the check that is never skipped. No omissions and no errors. Much harm comes not from dramatic disasters but from things that should have happened and did not.

Here, hospitals can be highly reliable. The Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s white paper Improving the Reliability of Health Care (2004) found that many care processes failed around one time in ten, and showed how well-designed processes can do far better: standardise and simplify, make the right action the default, build in reminders and checks, and add an independent second check where the stakes are highest. Reliability in this sense can be measured, as a failure rate tracked month by month, so a redesign can be tested for real change rather than a good month.

Reliable under pressure: coping with the unexpected

The high reliability research was about something different: staying safe in hazardous work where the unexpected is bound to happen. Hospitals face this too, with unpredictable demand, great variation between patients and many teams working together, so the five habits have to be adapted to healthcare rather than copied from aircraft carriers.

The two meanings support each other. Reliable routine care frees attention for the unexpected, and the habits of high reliability catch the failures that even good routines miss. Mark Chassin and Jerod Loeb’s High-Reliability Health Care: Getting There from Here (2013) set out how hospitals might work towards both, through leadership commitment, a safety culture and robust process improvement.

One caution: the label is easy and the habits are hard. “High reliability” can become a slogan on a poster rather than a change in how people notice, speak up and act.

A contrasting view: “normal accidents”

There is also a contrasting view. In Normal Accidents (1984), Charles Perrow argued that in some tightly coupled, complex systems, failures are bound to happen eventually, however well the system is run. The views are complementary: reduce complexity and coupling where you can, and practise the habits where you cannot.

How would you know it is working?

The habits leave traces in an organisation’s data: near-miss reporting rising as people trust the system; the time from a report to action falling; fewer repeat incidents of the same kind; more fixes that change the system rather than retrain people. Each can be tracked month by month and tested for real change rather than noise, for example with the free StepChange Analyzer.

And the final test is the one for any good habit: if the people who champion it left tomorrow, would it carry on? See leadership that lasts.

Questions people ask

What is a high reliability organisation?

An organisation that carries out complex, hazardous work, such as running an aircraft carrier, air traffic control or a nuclear power plant, with far fewer failures than would be expected. Researchers at the University of California, Berkeley began studying them in the 1980s to understand why things so rarely go wrong there.

What are the five principles of high reliability?

Karl Weick and Kathleen Sutcliffe described five: preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify, sensitivity to operations, commitment to resilience, and deference to expertise. The first three help an organisation anticipate problems; the last two help it contain them.

Can a hospital be a high reliability organisation?

In two senses. Hospitals can make routine care highly reliable, delivering known good practice every time without omissions or errors, through careful process design. And they can adopt the habits of high reliability to cope with the unexpected, though these have to be adapted to healthcare rather than copied from aircraft carriers.
