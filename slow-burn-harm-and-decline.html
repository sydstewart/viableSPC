Slow-burn harm and decline: when warnings become background

Not every major failure is sudden. Some build up over years, with warnings every day, and that is exactly why they get missed.

Sudden accidents are preceded by warning signs: near-misses, faulty instruments, similar events elsewhere (see major failures rarely come out of the blue). Two other kinds of failure follow the same pattern more slowly: slow-burn harm, such as years of poor care, and decline in an organisation’s performance or business. Their warnings are harder to see, not because they are rare, but because they are constant.

Slow-burn harm: when the warning is constant

Slow-burn failures are in some ways the most dangerous, because there is no single event to investigate. Patients cared for in corridors have poorer experiences, and the Royal College of Emergency Medicine has linked very long waits in emergency departments to excess deaths. Yet corridor care was tolerated for years, precisely because it happened every day: a warning that never stops becomes part of the background, “the way it is”. Some trusts have shown it does not have to be; see bright spots and evaluating corridor-care improvement.

Why it is so dangerous: there is no single event to investigate, so the warnings are never gathered in one place. Each day looks like the day before, and “the way it is” becomes the standard.

Decline: when the warnings are in the business figures

Organisations rarely collapse suddenly. Margins thin, customers drift away, a new technology or competitor gains ground, and each bad quarter has its own explanation. Kodak invented the digital camera but stayed with film; Nokia led mobile phones until the smartphone. The warnings were in the figures and in the market, and were explained away as temporary. See the innovator’s dilemma.

ICI, once Britain’s largest manufacturer, declined over two decades of restructuring before its takeover in 2008: decline usually takes years, with many chances to read the signs along the way.

For an organisation, the near-misses of decline are lost deals, stalled orders and complaints, and the measures that show them, such as quotes, orders and invoices month by month, can be checked for real change in exactly the same way as a safety measure.

How to see a slow-burn warning

  • Look at the measure over time, not month by month. A slow drift is invisible in a single result, but plain in a chart of several years.
  • Test whether the level has really shifted. A step change analysis shows whether a measure has moved to a new, worse level, and when, rather than just varying. See variation and SPC.
  • Ask a newcomer. People who arrive fresh notice what everyone else has stopped seeing.
  • Compare with others. If other organisations have found a way out of the same problem, it is not “the way it is”; see bright spots.
