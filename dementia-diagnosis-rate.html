The 66% Target: What the Dementia Diagnosis Data Actually Shows

England’s estimated diagnosis rate fell sharply after March 2020 and then partly recovered. The useful question is what changed in the pathway—not just whether a target was met.

By Syd Stewart · England · March observations, 2017–2025 · Not a current monthly update

The short answer

The linked CSV records 65.6% in March 2025: 1.1 percentage points below the 66.7% ambition. A diagnosis-rate target measures recorded coverage, not the quality of diagnosis, support or patient outcomes.

What the rate measures

The estimated dementia diagnosis rate (EDDR) compares recorded diagnoses among people aged 65 and over with the estimated number living with dementia. The denominator is modelled, not a count of everyone who actually has dementia.

Changes in recording, the registered population and prevalence assumptions can affect the result alongside changes in diagnostic activity. The rate is useful, but it cannot tell us by itself how long people wait, whether diagnoses are accurate or what support follows.

This example uses nine annual March observations from the existing downloadable CSV, attributed by the original article to OHID Fingertips. It is not the full monthly NHS series. Earlier historical claims and later monthly results should not be read into these nine points.

A sharp fall, followed by partial recovery

The CSV’s highest rate is 68.7% in 2019. It records 67.4% in March 2020 and 61.6% in March 2021—a fall of 5.8 percentage points. The rate then rises in each annual observation to 65.6% in March 2025.

The timing is consistent with pandemic disruption, but the series alone cannot separate changes in access, referrals, assessment capacity, recording or the denominator. Nor can it attribute the earlier higher rates to a particular policy or incentive.

What the CUSUM does—and does not—establish

New to CUSUM charts? CUSUM means cumulative sum: it adds each observation’s departure from a reference value. In these retrospective charts, that reference is the whole-series mean. What does the CUSUM line show?

One fitted stage means this run did not identify a boundary under its settings. It does not prove there was no important change, that the system is returning to normal, or that detection is impossible at every other setting. Nine annual observations provide limited information.

The green line accumulates deviations from the whole-series mean; its return towards zero is a mathematical consequence of centring, not evidence of recovery. A confidence setting is not the probability that a policy worked. Neither chart identifies a cause.

For local improvement, use a consistent, more frequent series where available, check definitions and dependence between observations, and record pathway changes alongside results.

Investigate the pathway, then test the change

Memory-clinic capacity, referral quality, workforce availability and access to assessment are plausible places to investigate. This national rate does not establish which is the binding constraint in a particular service. Talk to patients, carers and staff, and follow the work before choosing a solution.

  • Access and flow: referral volumes, waiting time to assessment and completed assessments.
  • Quality and fairness: diagnostic review, referral conversion and differences between population groups.
  • Support and outcomes: access to post-diagnostic support, patient and carer experience, and crisis care.
  • Balancing effects: workload, cost and whether faster throughput compromises assessment or support.

State what the intervention should change, why and over what timescale. Monitor the intended benefit and possible harm. Do not extrapolate a date for reaching the national target from this short recovery segment.

How to take this further

  • Right Measures — distinguish a coverage target from useful care and outcomes.
  • Right Problem — find the local constraint rather than assume it.
  • Right Intervention — test a response matched to the pathway evidence.
