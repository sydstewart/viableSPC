Understanding variation

What is the pattern telling us—and what response fits?

Every process varies. One good or bad result is not enough to judge an improvement. Plot observations in time order, understand how they were collected and use a method suited to the measure and its patterns.

Common causes and special causes

  • Common-cause variation: the variation expected from the current process when it is statistically stable. If its performance is unacceptable, improve the way the system works rather than adjusting it after every fluctuation.
  • Special-cause variation: evidence that the process is behaving differently from its established pattern. Investigate what changed; the chart signals a question, not the answer. Understand harmful changes and learn from beneficial ones—not every special cause should be eliminated.

A predictable process is not necessarily a good or safe one. An unpredictable process may need immediate containment as well as investigation. A serious incident can require action after one event; do not wait for a statistical signal to respond to harm.

Use a chart that fits the question

A run chart shows observations over time and can use defined rules to flag patterns. Statistical process control (SPC) charts add limits calculated for a particular type of measure and process.

Choose the chart and rules to suit the data—for example, individual measurements, proportions or event counts. Check denominators, changing definitions, seasonality and dependence between successive observations. Control limits describe expected process behaviour under the method’s assumptions; they are not targets or safety limits.

Step-change analysis looks for possible changes in average level. No detected step does not prove statistical stability or common-cause-only variation. A detected step does not identify its cause or prove an intervention worked. Different methods answer different questions.

Avoid tampering—not deliberate improvement

Tampering means adjusting a process in response to routine variation as though each result has a distinct cause. Repeated reactive adjustments can add variation and obscure what is happening.

That is different from a planned test to improve an unacceptable system. Make a prediction, check implementation and possible harm, and agree when to review. Do not keep changing the intervention simply because the latest result is disappointing.

Could the pattern be seasonal?

Recurring patterns—by season, weekday or time of day—can make before-and-after comparisons misleading. A winter-to-spring improvement may reflect a usual cycle, an intervention, or both.

Examine comparable periods across enough cycles and use an approach that accounts for seasonality. One same-month comparison is a useful check, not proof. Do not assume a standard control chart or shuffled-data change-point test automatically handles seasonal patterns.

Could an extreme result be followed by a more typical one?

When cases or periods are selected because an observed result is unusually high or low, later measurements may be less extreme because some of the original extremeness reflected random variation. This is regression to the mean—not a guarantee about the next observation.

A programme launched after an exceptionally bad period may receive credit for that return. Use a longer history, an advance prediction and a credible comparison where feasible. A prediction or change-point test alone does not rule this explanation out.

Could this be a false alarm?

A statistical method can signal a change that is not a real process change. Repeatedly trying different periods, measures, thresholds or rules increases opportunities to find a misleading result.

Choose the approach in advance where possible, record settings, check assumptions and investigate the context. No chart eliminates false alarms or missed changes. Operational or safety concerns still matter even without a signal.
