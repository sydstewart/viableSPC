Warnings at the moment of decision

Some of the most important warnings come at the start: when we choose the market, the product, or how to organise. Risks are there from the beginning, even when we hedge our bets.

Most of what is written about warning signs concerns things already running: near-misses, drifting measures, complaints (see major failures rarely come out of the blue). But many failures are set in motion much earlier, when a big decision is made. A plant built for a market that is already saturated, a product about to be overtaken by a cheaper alternative, or an organisation designed around conditions that are changing, can be in trouble from the day it opens, however well it is then run.

Three places to look before committing

  • Market: is demand growing, flat or saturated? Is there spare capacity elsewhere that could be turned against us?
  • Product or technology: could something else replace it? In the 1970s, for example, nylon fibre lost ground to polyester, which needed less energy to make.
  • Organisation design: is it set up to compete on cost, quality and reliability, or does it depend on things outside its control, such as raw materials, a single customer or a key person?

Good people and good operations cannot fully make up for a weak position chosen at the start. That is why this belongs with the right problem question: excellent work on the wrong problem does not save us.

Three habits that help

1. Imagine the failure in advance

Gary Klein’s pre-mortem asks the team to imagine that, some years from now, the decision has failed, and to write down why. It brings out the warnings people are reluctant to raise once a decision has momentum, and it makes doubt a contribution rather than disloyalty.

2. Hedge where you cannot know

Adrian Slywotzky, in The Upside, argues that many strategic risks can be anticipated and countered, not just suffered. One counter is double betting: when we cannot know which of two technologies or approaches will win, back more than one until the market shows the way. Hedging reduces the risk; it does not remove it.

3. Decide now what would make you change course

Before committing, agree the early-warning measures and the trigger points: for example, “if orders stay below a set level for six months, we review the decision”. Then, when the warning comes, it already has an agreed meaning, and is much harder to explain away.

A warning agreed in advance is a warning that cannot easily be explained away.

After the decision: keep watching

The warnings do not end when the decision is made; they become things to watch. Track the measures you chose month by month, and check whether a change is real before acting on it, or dismissing it.
