Ipswich — Making Ward Delays Visible and Acting on Them

The patient-flow approach described at Ipswich brings discharge planning, earlier decisions and visible delays into everyday ward work. Its value depends on how teams use the information—not simply on completing a bundle.

What was reported

The original case account describes improved discharge-before-noon rates and length-of-stay trends at Ipswich Hospital, part of East Suffolk and North Essex NHS Foundation Trust. It discusses the approach alongside the nationally promoted SAFER patient-flow bundle.

The material presented here does not include effect sizes, a complete dataset or direct evidence of reduced corridor-care exposure. Earlier discharge is a useful process indicator, but is not itself proof of safer care or less crowding.

What changed in practice

The account combines several ward-level practices:

  • Early review and board rounds: bringing relevant staff together to identify what each patient needs next and who will act.
  • Discharge planning from admission: setting and revisiting an expected discharge date, involving patients and families, and reviewing longer stays.
  • Red/Green Day tracking: making days with progress and avoidable delay visible, then investigating and addressing the reason. A label alone does not explain the delay or establish that discharge is safe.
  • Criteria-led discharge: enabling appropriately authorised staff to discharge patients when agreed clinical criteria are met, within local governance and escalation arrangements.

These practices are related but should not be treated as a verified six-element definition of SAFER. Use current official guidance and local clinical arrangements when implementing a bundle.

Why this matters

Pressure at the emergency department can reflect delays elsewhere in the hospital. Making the next clinical or discharge step visible helps teams distinguish a decision delay from missing treatment, transport or community support.

The transferable question is: can the people doing the work see what is preventing progress and obtain timely action? Copying a board-round time, colour code or discharge target without the necessary authority and capacity may add administration without improving flow. See Visual Management for the supporting concept.

What remains unproven

Several changes were introduced together, so any improvement cannot simply be attributed to one component. National promotion of an approach is not proof of its effects at Ipswich, its origin there or successful replication in every setting.

  • Outcomes: do length of stay and corridor-care exposure improve, rather than only the timing of discharge?
  • Safety: what happens to readmissions, patient experience and staff workload?
  • Persistence: are useful reviews and escalation maintained when staffing or leadership changes?

The account refers to run charts but does not provide a pre-committed Bootstrap CUSUM evaluation. Choose analysis appropriate to the data; do not treat one method as a requirement for learning. Check whether the intended practices were actually used, and consider other explanations for changes in results.

How to take this further

  • Bright Spot Analysis — investigate the mechanism and test transferability.
  • Corridor Care 2029 — connect local learning with evaluation of wider programmes.
  • PSIRF and Systemic Learning — connect patient-safety concerns, responses and evaluation.
