Gloucestershire — Improving Patient Flow Under Rising Demand

Gloucestershire reported substantial improvements in ambulance handover and discharge-related flow. The case asks how leadership, coordination and attention to harm supported change—and what the data can establish.

What was reported

In a June 2026 presentation, Chief Executive Kevin McNamara described improvements at Gloucestershire Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust. The reported figures include:

  • Ambulance handover delay: monthly hours fell from 3,343 in 2023 to 1,106 in 2025, reported as a 68% reduction.
  • Average handover time: fell from 80 minutes in 2022/23 to 26 minutes in 2025/26.
  • Discharge-ready patients: the daily average fell from 169 in 2023 to 118 in 2026.

Emergency attendances also rose across the periods presented. That makes the improvements worth investigating, but differing baselines and follow-up periods matter. Handover and discharge indicators are relevant to corridor care; they do not, by themselves, demonstrate its elimination.

What changed in practice

The presentation describes six connected priorities:

  • Focus on harm: make the consequences of poor flow visible, rather than treating performance targets as the whole purpose.
  • Reset partner relationships: coordinate flow across departments and organisations.
  • Question assumptions about beds: investigate whether additional beds alone would resolve the actual delays, without assuming capacity is irrelevant.
  • Connect quality, safety and flow: make their relationship explicit in executive responsibilities.
  • Clarify expectations: align roles and responsibilities from clinical specialties to the board.
  • Provide clinical leadership: sustain attention to flow and the authority to resolve problems.

These are the reported priorities, not separately demonstrated causes. Their value depends on what changed in everyday decisions, cooperation and capacity.

Why this matters

The useful mechanism to investigate is how shared information and accountability enabled coordinated action. Flow and safety should be evaluated together, not assumed to be competing aims or automatically compatible.

The remaining discharge-ready patients are also important. Their count shows continuing delay, but does not identify how much is due to internal processes, community capacity or other causes. Follow those patients’ pathways before diagnosing the constraint. The Joiner Triangle provides a framework for considering quality, evidence and teamwork together.

What remains unproven

The presentation includes time-series charts. Visual patterns can motivate formal analysis, but do not establish a change-point date or causal explanation. Rising demand does not rule out regression to the mean, reporting changes or other influences.

  • Evaluate the series: examine definitions, seasonality, activity and intervention dates using suitable methods.
  • Check safety broadly: a falling mortality indicator is encouraging, but is not proof that no harm was displaced. Include readmissions, incidents, experience and workload.
  • Test sustainability: do shared roles, partner cooperation and clinical leadership remain effective through financial pressure and personnel changes?

A prospective evaluation plan was not supplied with the case account. A retrospective analysis can still be useful, provided it is labelled honestly. A change point alone cannot isolate the effects of six simultaneous changes.

How to take this further

  • Bright Spot Analysis — investigate the mechanism and test transferability.
  • Corridor Care 2029 — connect local learning with evaluation of wider programmes.
  • PSIRF and Systemic Learning — connect patient-safety concerns, responses and evaluation.
