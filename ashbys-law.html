Variation, demand and capacity

Can the system cope with the work—and does the intervention leave room to respond?

Average demand and average capacity can look balanced while people still wait. Work arrives unevenly, jobs take different amounts of time, and the skills or resources needed are not always available together.

Look beyond the averages

Imagine a service with enough time to handle an average day’s orders. Requests cluster in the morning, some need specialist help, and urgent work interrupts planned work. A quiet afternoon cannot undo every delay created earlier.

  • Amount: how much work arrives, including rework and unmet demand?
  • Timing: when does it arrive, how long does it take and when is capacity available?
  • Variety: what different needs, skills and responses are involved?

Follow the work with the people doing it. Check these patterns over time and at the steps where queues form—not just in organisation-wide totals. Find what is limiting the result.

Keep capacity to respond

Utilisation is the proportion of available capacity being used. Slack is capacity not already committed, which can help absorb variation and recover from disruption.

When arrivals and job times vary, waiting can rise sharply as utilisation approaches full capacity. Keeping everyone busy is not the same as helping work finish promptly.

Around 80% utilisation can be a useful planning prompt—not a universal limit. There is no fixed cliff at 80%, and 20% slack is not a rule for every resource. The margin needed depends on variability, pooling of resources and the consequences of delay.

Protect useful flexibility where and when it is needed, with the right skills. Do not fill every gap simply to improve a utilisation score—or assume spare capacity in one team can relieve a constraint elsewhere.

Design and test the arrangement

  • Reduce avoidable variation. Remove rework, missing information and artificial batches; standardise routine work without ignoring genuine differences in need.
  • Match capacity to demand. Consider flexible schedules, shared capacity or cross-training where safe and practical. Protect a clear route for urgent work and exceptions.
  • Keep a deliberate margin. Agree how response capacity will be protected and used. If demand persistently exceeds capacity, slack alone is not a solution: address capacity, scope or demand with those affected.
  • Check the whole result. Monitor completed work, waiting, quality, safety, workload and cost. Did the change improve flow or simply transfer the queue or burden?
How Ashby’s Law helps explain this

Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety concerns the range of responses needed to manage the range of disturbances that matter to a system’s goal.

For a service, a useful question is: can our arrangements respond to the different needs we actually encounter? Reduce avoidable complexity and build appropriate ways to handle what remains—for example, a clear routine route plus skilled judgement for exceptions.

Variety is not the same as numerical variability or workload. Extra hours alone may not help if the required skills, information or authority are missing. Nor does the law mean every difference should be standardised away.
